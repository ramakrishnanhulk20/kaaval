---
title: The trust model
---

Kaaval asks you to trust a record. This page says exactly how much of it you have to take on faith,
and what you can check for yourself in two minutes.

## What the record is

A simulated paper record. No real funds, no live orders. Three paper accounts, each starting from
the balance written into the config entry of the run, trading against order books read from Bitget
at the instant of each trade.

Crypto hedge legs can run on Bitget's demo environment when a demo key is present. Stock legs
cannot, because the demo environment has no stock symbols. Those fills are produced by our
simulator.

The word simulated is on the scoreboard, the timeline, the trade log and the proof panel, and in the
ledger itself: every mark entry carries `simulated: true` and the opening config entry carries the
note "every fill in this ledger is simulated".

## What signing and replay prove

**The chain proves the record was not edited after the fact.** Every entry hashes its own content
together with the hash of the entry before it, and the hash is signed with an Ed25519 key whose
private half lives outside the repo. Change one character in one entry and `npm run verify:ledger`
names that entry. Delete an entry and the sequence numbers say so. Reorder two and the previous
hashes say so. Re-sign the whole file with a new key and the public key no longer matches the one
published in the README and on the proof panel.

**Replay proves every fill follows from a real book.** Each fill carries the hash of the order book
it hit, and that book is stored in the ledger as a snapshot entry. `npx tsx scripts/replay.ts`
recomputes each fill from that snapshot, at the fee rates and slippage model recorded before it, and
compares field by field. A fill that could not have happened on that book comes back as a mismatch.

**The attack script proves the risk layer is not talkable.** Sixteen hostile scenarios go to a brain
that obeys whatever a headline tells it, with no network, no model and no keys in the process. Ten
are refused, two are cut down, and four controls are allowed through.

## What they do not prove

- **They do not prove the fill model is right.** Replay shows every fill follows from the model and
  from a real recorded book. The model is still ours.
- **The resting-limit assumption.** A limit order that does not cross the spread is treated as filled
  at its own price. On a thin rToken book that is optimistic: in the real world you join a queue and
  may not be filled at all. The cap of a quarter of the visible book keeps the error small. It does
  not make the assumption true. This is the one place where the record flatters itself, and it is
  written into the fill model's own header rather than buried.
- **They do not explain a decision.** The schema and the rulebook bound what a decision can do. The
  rationale a model writes is its own account of itself. Kaaval publishes it without endorsing it.
- **Divergence is measured against Bitget's own last regular close**, not the NYSE print, because
  Kaaval reads only Bitget. The two are usually the same number at the bell and can differ by a few
  basis points.
- **One machine.** The engine runs on one PC. A reboot shows as a gap in the timestamps. The hash
  chain carries across the gap; the clock does not, and the site draws the hole rather than smoothing
  the line over it.

## Why the record starts where it starts

The first entry in the published ledger is dated 2026-09-11T08:21:28Z. Everything before that was
development on throwaway ledgers. There are two gaps in it, both visible in the timestamps: 21
minutes on 11 September, 09:45Z to 10:06Z, and 18 hours 33 minutes from 11 September 11:23Z to 12
September 05:56Z, while the machine that runs the engine was off.

Showing the gaps is the point. A record that cannot show you its own holes is not a record.

The full list of what is not fixed is in the [threat model](/docs/security/threat-model).
