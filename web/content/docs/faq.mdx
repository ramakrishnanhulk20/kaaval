## Is the money real?

No. Kaaval holds no funds and places no live order. Three paper accounts start from the balance in
the run's config entry and trade against order books read from Bitget at the instant of each trade.
The word simulated is on the scoreboard, the timeline, the trade log and the proof panel, and every
`mark` entry in the ledger carries `simulated: true`.

The one place real infrastructure is touched is Bitget's demo trading environment, which has no real
money either. When a demo key is present the crypto hedge legs are placed there for real and
Bitget's response is written into the record.

## Why simulated, if the point is a real record?

Because there is no paper venue for a tokenized stock. Bitget's demo environment has exactly three
contracts, `SBTCSUSDT`, `SETHSUSDT` and `SXRPSUSDT`, and no stock symbols at all. A live record
would need real money on real rTokens.

So the choice was between a simulator that is fully checkable and a live record that needs funding.
Kaaval took the first and made it as checkable as it could: the book every fill hit is stored beside
the fill, and anyone can recompute the fill from the book. See the
[trust model](/docs/concepts/trust-model) for the part of it you still have to take on faith.

## Why three brains?

Because one model with one opinion proves nothing. All three see the identical world state, run
under the identical rulebook and trade the identical sizes, so a difference between them is a
difference in judgement and nothing else.

The third one, `rules`, carries no model at all: basis mean reversion between an rToken and its
perpetual, plus the weekend hedge. It is the honest baseline. If the language-model brains cannot
beat a published rule set on the same night, the record says so, and that is a result worth
publishing too.

## Why is there a gap on 11 September?

Two gaps, and both are in the record on purpose.

The first is 21 minutes on 11 September, 09:45Z to 10:06Z. The second is 18 hours 33 minutes, from
11 September 11:23Z to 12 September 05:56Z, while the machine that runs the engine was off after a
reboot.

The hash chain carries across a gap: the entry after it points at the entry before it, and the
verifier is happy. The clock does not carry across, and the site draws the hole rather than
smoothing a line over it. A record that cannot show you its own holes is not a record.

## Can I run it without keys?

Yes, for most of it. Every price, book, candle and instrument call goes to Bitget's public market
data and needs no credentials, so `npm test`, `npm run attack`, `npx tsx scripts/replay.ts` and a
dry-run tick all work on a fresh clone.

What needs a key: the Claude brain needs `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY`, the Qwen brain needs `QWEN_API_KEY`,
company news and the earnings calendar need `FINNHUB_API_KEY`, and the demo hedge legs need the
three demo variables. A brain with no key is skipped and the log says so, every tick. See the
[environment page](/docs/api/environment).

## What does Qwen do?

The same thing Claude does, through a different model. The Qwen brain gets the identical world
state, the identical rulebook text and the identical JSON schema, over Bitget's own hackathon proxy
at `https://hackathon.bitgetops.com/v1`, model `qwen3.8-max`.

It is there as a controlled comparison: two models, one prompt, one set of facts. As of the run
recorded in the README on 12 September 2026 it is wired and skipped, because `QWEN_API_KEY` is not
set. The engine log says so on every tick rather than pretending it ran.

## How do I know a fill really happened?

You cannot know that it happened on an exchange, because it did not: the stock legs are simulated.
What you can check is that it follows from a real order book that Bitget served at that moment.

Each fill carries the hash of the book it was priced against, and that book is in the ledger as a
snapshot entry. `npx tsx scripts/replay.ts` recomputes every fill from that snapshot, at the fee
rates and slippage model recorded before it, and compares quantity, average price, notional, fee,
slippage and levels consumed. A fill that could not have happened on that book comes back as a
mismatch with its sequence number.

For the demo-environment legs the answer is different: Bitget matched those, they are flagged
`demo: true`, and the ledger holds the request and the response.

## What happens on a reboot?

pm2 brings the engine back, the state file restores every position, balance and peak equity, and the
ledger chains straight on from the entry before the restart.

The record shows the outage as a gap in the timestamps, which is what happened. The 18 hour 33
minute gap on 11 and 12 September is exactly this: the PC rebooted and the process manager was not
yet set to start on logon.

## What is not covered?

Said plainly, in one place:

- The fill model is ours. Replay proves every fill follows from it and from a real book, not that
  the model is right.
- A resting limit order is assumed filled at its own price, which flatters a thin book. The cap of a
  quarter of the visible book bounds the error; it does not remove it.
- Divergence is measured against Bitget's own last regular close, not the NYSE print.
- The rationale a model writes is its own account of itself. It is published, not endorsed.
- The engine runs on one machine, so its availability is one machine's availability.
- Nobody outside the team has audited any of this. See the [self-audit](/docs/security/self-audit).

## Where is the six-field log?

`data/state/trades.csv` on the engine machine, and `/api/trades.csv` on the site, which downloads as
`kaaval-trades-simulated.csv`.

It carries timestamp, instrument, direction, price, quantity, balance change and balance after, plus
which brain traded and a note with the fee, the slippage and the hash of the book. The engine appends
to it as fills happen, and `npx tsx scripts/export-log.ts` rebuilds the whole file from the signed
ledger, so it is reproducible from the record rather than a file you have to trust.
